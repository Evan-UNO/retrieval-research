# Research meeting review and rehearsal

Prepare a presentation from existing reading, notes, code checks, and experiment records. Follow the user's duration and advisor requirements. If unspecified, produce a short outline rather than a full slide deck.

## Organize the material

1. Establish this meeting's scope and available records. Distinguish paper-author conclusions, the learner's understanding or derivations, their actual experiments, and unexecuted plans. Failed runs, missing resources, and open questions are valid progress, not completed results.
2. Organize around one main question: why it matters, what was done, what the evidence shows, what remains uncertain, and how to test it next. A reading-only week can focus on method understanding and unresolved protocols without invented experiments.
3. Attach a source to each important claim: paper page/figure/table, note location, or experiment configuration/log/output. Label copied paper figures and author metrics as author results. Distinguish personal small-scale pipeline checks from the paper's full experiments.
4. Choose one feasible next task with a judgeable outcome, required materials, and deliverable. Preparing a meeting does not require inventing a new research project or promising experiments without resources.

Default output: `main question | 3-5 talking points with evidence | open questions | one next task`. Scale to the duration rather than retelling every paper section. Generate slides, a script, or charts when requested; use available presentation capabilities for slide decks while preserving evidence labels.

## Rehearsal and reuse

Offer two or three questions suitable for spoken answers: why a design is needed, how far a result supports a claim, or how the next experiment distinguishes explanations. Let the user answer before source-based feedback unless they request reference answers. Without answers, record "awaiting practice," not mastery.

Check reuse on reading-only, failed-experiment, and new-result weeks. The outline should reflect actual progress, trace each result to materials, retain unknown failure causes, and connect next steps to evidence. Fluent presentation does not replace factual accuracy.
